Why we teach kana

At fudami, we believe kana, as much as kanji, are part of learning Japanese. Here is why, with our sources. Where the evidence runs out, we say so.

More than half of written Japanese is kana

In 2022, Makoto Yamazaki of the National Institute for Japanese Language and Linguistics (NINJAL) counted the characters of the Balanced Corpus of Contemporary Written Japanese: 104.3 million words of books, magazines, newspapers, blogs, textbooks, laws and more. Hiragana made up 48.8% of the characters and katakana 7.1%, so about 56% were kana. Kanji made up 30.3%, and Latin letters 1.5%.

Even in newspapers, one of the registers richest in kanji, kana (44.9%) outnumber kanji (41.4%).

Kana hold the sentence together

Kanji write most nouns and the core of verbs and adjectives: they carry most of the meaning. Hiragana writes the particles, the endings and the small grammatical words; katakana writes borrowed words (Tanaka, 1980). In 猫が魚を食べた, “the cat ate the fish”, が, を and べた are hiragana.

What about romaji?

We found no study that follows learners who read Japanese through romaji and then tests how well they read kana or kanji later. The closest studies point both ways:

  • Adding romaji to hiragana did not help 61 first-semester students learn more words; the students who used the audio more remembered more (Okuyama, 2007).
  • Introducing kana 8 weeks later made no significant difference on two post-tests (Hatasa, 2002).
  • With 98 adults, romaji helped them recall the sound of new kanji words better than furigana did (Allen, 2023, master's thesis).

So this part is a belief, not a finding: Japanese is written in kana and kanji, so we teach them from the first day. If you already know words by ear, fudami keeps them, and they come back as soon as you can read them.

How long does it take?

There are 46 basic hiragana and 46 basic katakana, plus their voiced and combined forms. The Japan Foundation's free self-study courses plan about 14 hours of study for hiragana and 14 for katakana, to read and type them. That is a course plan, not a measurement: we found no study that measures how long learners take.

fudami teaches a few kana at a time and brings each one back just before you would forget it.

Does writing by hand help?

For a new script, it seems to:

  • 42 adults learned Arabic letters by typing them, looking at them or writing them. Those who wrote learned faster, with fewer trials, and did better on tasks they had not trained (Wiley & Rapp, 2021).
  • Adults who learned new characters by hand confused them less often with their mirror images than adults who typed them, mostly three weeks after training (Longcamp et al., 2006).

It also has a cost:

  • In a class of 52 learners of Chinese, hand-copying took 34.69% of practice time and lowered word recognition right after practice; one week later, the two conditions no longer differed (Lu et al., 2019).
  • In a first study of 5 learners of kanji, typing helped them recall readings and handwriting helped them recall forms (Sugaya, 2025).

None of these studies is about kana, so we promise no gain from writing them. That is why handwriting in fudami will always be free and optional, with typing always available.
